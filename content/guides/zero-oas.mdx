A job search is a funnel. Applications go in, and a smaller number come out at each stage: a response or online assessment (OA), then an interview, then an offer. When the search isn't working, the useful question isn't "what's wrong with me?" It's "**which stage is losing people?**"

Each stage fails for different reasons, so each one needs a different fix. Working on the wrong stage is the most common way students waste a month.

## Step 1: count your results

Open your tracker, or your inbox if you don't have one, and count for the last batch of applications:

| Stage | Count |
| --- | --- |
| Applications sent | |
| Responses or OAs | |
| Interviews | |
| Offers | |

You need a reasonable batch before the numbers mean much. Twenty to thirty applications to the same kind of role is a good minimum. Five is too few to tell anything.

## Step 2: find the first stage where it drops to zero

### Lots of applications, no responses or OAs

This is the most common problem, and the cause is almost always before anyone reads your resume carefully.

Check these in order:

1. **Are you applying to roles you match?** Compare five of the postings you applied to against your resume. If most ask for skills or experience you don't have, the problem is targeting, not the resume. Read [Pick one target before you apply anywhere](/guides/pick-one-target).
2. **Does the top third of your resume make the case?** A reviewer skims. Your most relevant project or experience should be visible without scrolling, with a result attached.
3. **Can software read it?** Many companies use applicant tracking systems that extract text from your resume. Multi-column layouts, text in images, tables and unusual fonts can come out scrambled. Copy your PDF text into a plain text editor. If it comes out garbled, rebuild it in a simple one-column layout.
4. **Are you applying late?** Popular postings can collect a large number of applicants in the first days. Applying in the first week helps.

### OAs, but no interviews

Your resume is doing its job. The OA isn't.

- Most OAs for SWE roles are timed coding problems. Practise under a timer, in the language you'll use, without looking things up.
- Practise the problem types that show up for your target, not random ones. Arrays, strings, hash maps, two pointers, trees and graphs cover a lot of internship OAs.
- Read the instructions for each OA carefully. Some also test debugging, SQL or short written answers.

### Interviews, but no offers

You're close. The work now is communication as much as code.

- Practise talking while you solve. Interviewers score how you reason, not just the final answer.
- Prepare three or four stories from your projects and jobs that you can tell in two minutes each: the problem, what you did, and what changed.
- After each interview, write down every question you remember. Patterns show up quickly.

## Step 3: change one thing, then run another batch

Pick the fix for the stage that's failing and change only that. Then send another focused batch and count again. If you change your resume, your target and your prep all at once, you won't know which one worked.

## A worked example

> **Target:** SWE internship, Summer 2027
> **Sent:** 40 · **OAs:** 0 · **Interviews:** 0 · **Offers:** 0

The first zero is at OAs, so the problem is before the resume gets a fair read. Checking the postings shows half of them asked for 2+ years of experience. That's a targeting problem. The fix: filter to roles that are explicitly for students, move the strongest project to the top of the resume with a measurable result, and send the next 25.

This is exactly the kind of diagnosis Career OS coaches do in a session, using your real application data instead of guesses.
