Most students start their search by opening a job board and applying to everything with "intern" in the title. It feels productive. It also makes every other part of the search harder, because you can't tell whether your resume is good when you don't know what it's supposed to be good *for*.

A target fixes that. It's one role and one season, written down: **"Machine learning internship, Winter 2027"** or **"New-grad backend role, Summer 2027."** Everything else gets measured against it.

## Why one target works better than ten

**Your resume can only argue one case well.** A resume aimed at ML roles puts your model projects first and your math courses near the top. A resume aimed at frontend roles leads with shipped UI and design work. Trying to cover both usually means neither reader finds what they're looking for in the first few lines.

**Your results become readable.** If you send 30 applications to one kind of role and get no responses, that tells you something specific. If you send 30 applications to six kinds of roles, the silence could mean anything.

**Your prep time goes further.** Interview prep for data roles (SQL, statistics, case questions) looks very different from prep for SWE roles (data structures, system design basics). One target means one prep plan.

## How to choose

Answer these four questions in order.

### 1. Which season?

Internships in North America usually run in fixed terms. If your school uses co-op terms, you'll often see Winter (January to April), Summer (May to August) and Fall (September to December). Summer is the most competitive because every student is available. Winter and Fall terms often have fewer applicants per role.

Recruiting opens well before the term starts. Many larger tech companies post summer internships in the late summer and fall of the year before. Pick the season first, then work backwards to find out when you need to be ready to apply.

### 2. Which role family?

Pick the one where you already have the most evidence: projects, courses, a previous job. Common families for CS students are:

- Software engineering (backend, frontend, full-stack, mobile)
- Machine learning and AI
- Data (analyst, data science, data engineering)
- Infrastructure and DevOps

If two feel equal, pick the one with more open roles for your season. You can change later. The point is to have one at a time.

### 3. How far away are you?

Read ten real postings for that role and season. Write down every requirement that shows up in at least half of them. Then mark each one: **have it**, **partly**, or **missing**.

If most items are "have it", you're ready to apply. If most are "missing", pick a closer role for this season and treat the harder one as your next target.

### 4. Where can you work?

Write down the cities you'll work in and whether you'll relocate. This sounds minor, but it removes a surprising number of postings, and it stops you from spending an evening on applications you'd turn down anyway.

## Write it down

Put your target somewhere you'll see it every week:

> **Target:** ML internship · Winter 2027 · Toronto or remote
> **Ready to apply by:** October 15
> **Biggest gaps:** no deployed project, PyTorch not on resume

Every decision after this gets easier. Should you take that extra course? Does this project belong on your resume? Should you apply to this posting? Check it against the target.

## When to change your target

Change it when your results give you a reason, not when you feel impatient. Two good reasons:

- You've sent a focused batch of applications (20 or more), fixed the obvious resume problems, and still get no responses. The gap may be bigger than it looked.
- The season has passed or the postings have closed.

Change on a feeling and you're back to applying to everything.
