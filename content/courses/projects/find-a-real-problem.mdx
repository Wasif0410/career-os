## Start with someone, not a technology stack

A project becomes easier to explain when you know who it is for and which task it improves. Begin with a problem you can observe: organizing club equipment, comparing accessible study spaces or finding mistakes in a public dataset. You do not need a new startup idea.

Watch the current process or ask someone about a recent example. Ask what they did, where the process became difficult and what they tried. Avoid starting with “Would you use my app?” A polite yes gives you less information than a specific account of existing behavior.

Choose one user and one workflow. Write what the first version will do and what it will leave out. Decide how you will know whether it helps. A small project tested against a clear question is more instructive than a large list of unfinished features.

## A worked example

Maya notices a club tracks equipment in a spreadsheet. Members lose time finding whether an item is available. Her first brief is: “Help a club organizer look up an item and see its last recorded status.” Payments, recommendations and a mobile app are out of scope.

She will test whether a few volunteers can find sample items and understand their status. She does not claim the club wants to replace its spreadsheet before discussing the idea with them.

## Your turn · 45–60 minutes

1. List three problems from a class, job, hobby or community.
2. Investigate one current workflow through observation or a willing participant.
3. Write a one-page brief: user, problem, current process, first workflow, exclusions and evaluation question.
4. Sketch the smallest prototype that could test your assumption.

**Leave with:** a scoped project brief. A remote conversation or your own well-documented experience can be a starting point when access is limited; label assumptions you have not validated. Use consent and synthetic data rather than collecting personal information unnecessarily.
