## Reach out with a reason to learn

A good conversation request makes the purpose clear and leaves the other person room to decline. Start with someone whose work or career path relates to a question you have. A shared class, community or interest can provide context, but it does not create an obligation to respond.

Read enough about their work to ask a specific question. Keep the first message brief. If you request a call, propose a short conversation and offer a written alternative. Do not attach a long demand for referrals or send the same message to a large list of people.

An informational conversation is for investigating a field. A connection count is not a measure of how much you learned. One thoughtful exchange can help you refine an assumption.

## A worked example

> Hi Sam, I'm a CS student exploring software for public transport. Your post about route data quality raised a question for my student project: how does your team notice when an upstream feed changes? If you're open to it, I'd appreciate a brief conversation or a reply here. No worries if your schedule is full.

This original template names context and a question. Personalize it only with things you actually read. If someone agrees, prepare two or three questions, listen and end at the agreed time.

## Your turn · 20 minutes, plus the conversation

1. Identify one person and one question you want to investigate.
2. Draft a short request in your own words.
3. Check that it is respectful and easy to decline.
4. If you choose to send it yourself, record what you learned and thank them for their time.

**Leave with:** a personal outreach draft and a learning question. Sending is optional. A campus group, office hours or a public technical discussion can be an alternative. If you do follow up, keep it brief and respect a decline or continued non-response.
