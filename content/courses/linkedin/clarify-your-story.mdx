## Help people understand where you are going

Your headline and About section should give someone a clear starting point for a conversation. Describe your current context, the kind of work you are exploring and a concrete interest or experience. You can be early in your learning without presenting yourself as an expert.

Write the About section in your own voice. Use a short sequence: what interests you, what you have worked on, what you learned and what you want to explore next. Avoid a list of personality adjectives that could describe anyone. Keep dates and project descriptions consistent with your resume.

Your profile does not need to appeal to every possible employer. Clarity is more helpful than stacking unrelated job titles. You can change the focus as your interests develop.

## A worked example

Headline: “CS student exploring backend engineering · Building tools for campus organizations.”

About opening: “I like making routine work easier for people. In a class project, I built an API for tracking club equipment and learned how much clear error messages matter. I'm now practicing database testing and looking for opportunities to learn how small engineering teams work.”

This is an original fictional example. It gives someone a reason to ask about the API and accurately signals that the student is still learning. It does not promise more profile views or preferential search placement.

## Your turn · 20 minutes

1. Draft two headlines with different emphasis: role and domain.
2. Write a short About section using one real example.
3. Delete claims you cannot support and phrases you would never say aloud.
4. Ask a peer what kind of work they think you want to explore.

**Leave with:** a headline and About draft. If the peer's answer is unrelated to your intention, simplify. Save the draft before publishing; there is no need to update your public profile until you are comfortable with its accuracy and visibility.
