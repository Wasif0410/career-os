## Plan outputs, not ambition

A plan is useful when it fits your real week. Start with classes, paid work, rest and other responsibilities already on your calendar. Then decide how much time you can sustainably spend. More hours are not automatically a better plan.

Choose one outcome that addresses the gap in your evidence map. Break it into a small experiment, a deliverable and a review. Define “done” before starting so you can stop polishing. Include someone who can give feedback, or a test you can repeat yourself.

Keep the rest of your goals on a parking list. If a task is too large for the time available, shrink its scope. You can test one endpoint instead of rebuilding your whole project. You can rewrite three bullets instead of redesigning every document.

## A worked example

Maya has four hours available across two weeks. Her output is a tested API feature, not “become a better backend engineer.” She schedules:

- Session 1: reproduce an invalid-input bug and describe expected behavior.
- Session 2: add validation and tests for valid, missing and malformed input.
- Session 3: update the README with setup and an example request.
- Session 4: ask a classmate to run the feature from a fresh checkout; fix one issue they find.

Her definition of done is a runnable feature, test evidence and a short explanation of the tradeoff. If deployment takes longer than expected, she can share a recorded local demo and describe that limitation.

## Your turn · 20 minutes

1. Name one deliverable and the gap it addresses.
2. List your available sessions. Add a realistic buffer for setup or feedback.
3. Write a three-part definition of done: what works, how you will check it, and what someone can inspect.
4. Schedule a review and a smaller fallback version.

**Leave with:** a two-week plan you can put on your calendar. If completing it requires skipping essential responsibilities, revise the scope. The exercise is about consistency and learning, not proving that you can stay busy.
