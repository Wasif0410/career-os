## Turn understanding into a small, useful experiment

Look for a connection between what you learned and a skill you want to practice. The result might be a clearer instruction sheet, a data check, an accessibility improvement or a small prototype. Sometimes the best next step is another conversation or a simpler process, rather than software.

Write a hypothesis: “I think this change could help this person complete this task because of this observation.” Define how you will check it. Ask permission before changing a community's process, and make clear who will maintain the result. A student experiment should not quietly become someone's unsupported critical system.

Keep your learning broader than the artifact. You may have practiced explanation, coordination or judgment. Those experiences can contribute to an interview story without being inflated into professional domain expertise.

## A worked example

Maya creates a paper checklist for equipment returns before changing the app. She asks the organizer to review the wording and tries it with fictional scenarios. The experiment reveals that inspection responsibility needs to be defined first.

Her reflection connects a technical lesson to a domain lesson: modeling states is easier after understanding who can change them. She can now explain why her project includes “awaiting inspection” rather than a single availability flag.

## Your turn · one small experiment

1. Pick one observation from your field guide.
2. Propose the smallest change that could test your hypothesis.
3. Ask for feedback or use clearly labeled simulated scenarios.
4. Record the result, its limits and what you would investigate next.

**Leave with:** a domain field guide, a reflection and a tested idea. Keep an interest simply because you enjoy it if that is what you want. The point of going beyond your technical bubble is to understand more of the world and the people in it, not to turn every experience into a performance metric.
