## Let someone's experience change your model

A conversation can reveal details that public material leaves out. Seek a willing person who knows the workflow you are studying. Explain that you are learning, ask for a small amount of time and offer a written alternative. Respect a decline and do not record or share their words without permission.

Ask about a recent event. “Can you walk me through the last time an item came back damaged?” is more concrete than “What are your biggest pain points?” Follow the sequence: what happened, what they did, what information they used and what made the situation difficult.

Avoid leading questions that advertise your preferred solution. Listen for workarounds and exceptions. Afterward, separate their observations from your interpretation. One person offers one perspective; seek other perspectives before drawing broad conclusions.

## A worked example

Maya assumes the organizer needs faster search. During a conversation, she learns that unclear responsibility after a return causes more confusion. She writes two notes: “Organizer described waiting for inspection” and “Maybe ownership is more important than search speed.” The first is reported evidence; the second is her hypothesis.

She thanks the organizer and asks whether a summary of what she learned would be useful. She does not treat the conversation as permission to publish their name or internal records.

## Your turn · 20-minute conversation plus reflection

1. Prepare three open questions about a real event or workflow.
2. Invite a willing participant and explain the purpose.
3. Summarize what you heard and check your understanding.
4. Update the field guide with one changed assumption and one question that remains.

**Leave with:** an anonymized reflection and a better question. If a conversation is not available, study a public interview or documented case and label that limitation. Do not pretend that secondhand research is direct user research.
