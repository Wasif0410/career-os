## Give the reader something to evaluate

A useful bullet describes an action, enough context to understand it, and an outcome or check. Start with what you did. Name the object of the work and the reason it mattered. Add a measurement only when you can explain where it came from.

You do not need a percentage in every bullet. Scope, a specific capability or a test result can provide context. “Handled empty CSV files without crashing” is meaningful even without a revenue figure. If you did measure speed, keep the baseline, dataset and method in your notes.

Avoid claiming a team result as your individual result. Be explicit about the part you owned. If you used generated code, you still need to understand, test and explain the part you present as your work.

## A worked example

Weak: “Worked on an inventory website using React and SQL.”

Clearer: “Implemented item search and pagination for a class inventory app, using SQL queries and tests for empty and duplicate results.”

If actually measured, a different bullet could say: “Reduced median search response time from 240 ms to 110 ms on a 10,000-row local test dataset by adding an index.” These numbers are **fictional examples**, not numbers to paste into your resume. A local benchmark is not a production impact claim. If you did not measure, use the capability and validation instead.

## Your turn · 25 minutes

1. Pick three vague bullets from your outline.
2. Write the action, technical or operational context, and observed result for each.
3. Remove adjectives that do not add evidence.
4. Under each metric, record its source and conditions in your private notes.

**Leave with:** three bullets you can explain without reading them. Ask a peer “What did I do?” after they read one. If they cannot answer, reduce jargon or add context. If they assume a bigger result than you achieved, narrow the claim.
